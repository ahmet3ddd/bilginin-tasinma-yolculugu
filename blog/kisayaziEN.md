# How does a kilogram get lost?

West of Paris, in the Pavillon de Breteuil in the park of Saint-Cloud,
there is a basement. The vault inside it opens with three separate keys,
and the three keys are held by three different people; none of them can
get in alone. Inside the vault, under three nested glass bell jars, sits
a small cylinder of platinum-iridium alloy.

From 1889 to 2019, the kilogram **was that cylinder**. It did not
represent the kilogram, it defined it. Every scale on earth, every drug
dose, every engineering calculation traced back to it.

In 130 years it was weighed only three times: 1889, 1948, 1989.

The third time, a problem surfaced. The cylinder had drifted about
**50 micrograms** away from its official copies distributed around the
world. Roughly a fiftieth of an eyelash.

The size of the gap is not the point. The point is that nobody could
answer the obvious question: **which one changed?** Did the cylinder get
lighter, or did the copies get heavier? Answering that would require a
kilogram more trustworthy than the cylinder. No such thing existed. A
definition cannot measure itself.

On 20 May 2019 they solved it at the root. The kilogram stopped being an
object and was tied to a constant of nature: the Planck constant, exactly
6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. The kilogram can no longer burn, be stolen, wear
down, or be smudged by a fingerprint. The cylinder is still in that
vault, but it is now a museum piece, not a standard.

It looks like a perfect repair. Up to a point it is.

Because turning that number into a mass you can hold requires an
instrument called a **Kibble balance**. The number of laboratories that
can run one at the required precision does not fill one hand. The number
of people who can build and tune one is smaller still — and much of that
knowledge is not written down. It lives in the hands of the people
working in those labs.

So the fragility did not disappear. It **moved**. From an object to an
apparatus, and to the few pairs of hands that know how to operate it.

---

This is the one sentence behind the work I have spent the past year on:
**knowledge travels; context does not always travel with it.**

A recipe, a symbol, a standard — all of them can be written down, copied,
handed across generations. But the thing that **opens** them is not
always on the paper. Sometimes it is in a craftsman's hands, sometimes in
a machine, sometimes in an arrangement that stands only as long as
somebody keeps maintaining it.

I traced this through 32 real objects, one at a time: from the engraved
ochre of Blombos Cave to Iznik tiles, from Roman aqueducts to the Ubykh
language, from the Antikythera mechanism to the kilogram. I tested each one against six
links — packet, decoder, context, tacit knowledge, apparatus,
maintenance.

The result surprised me too. In **21 of the 32**, the broken link was one
of the same three: tacit knowledge, apparatus, maintenance. Which means
what is lost is almost never *the knowledge itself* — it is the
surroundings that made it usable.

A chain does not live as long as its strongest link. It lives as long as
its most neglected one.
